## The problem

In a company of 10 to 100 people, technology carries a lot of weight: the product, the website, back-office systems, internal tools, data, security. Technical decisions commit the company for years.

Yet technical leadership doesn’t always fill a week. A team of five developers needs someone to set direction, hire well, and settle architecture choices. It doesn’t need that person in meetings every day.

So the company faces a false choice: hire a CTO too early, or go without one for too long.

## What a full-time CTO costs

Salary is only part of it. Add:

- **payroll taxes, benefits, and bonus**;
- **equity**, which experienced candidates often expect;
- **recruiting**: search firm fees, leadership time, and months of searching;
- **the risk of getting it wrong**: a bad CTO hire shows after six months, and their decisions stay after they leave.

For a small company, it’s often the most expensive and riskiest hire it will make.

## What having no CTO costs

Without a CTO, technical decisions still get made. By default, by a founder with no time, by the longest-serving developer, or by a vendor whose interests lie elsewhere.

The consequences show up later, and cost more:

- an architecture that can’t handle growth and has to be rebuilt;
- total dependence on one developer or one agency;
- bad hires, because no one can properly evaluate candidates;
- vendors billing for work no one checks;
- security holes found by a customer or an attacker;
- a due diligence that lowers the valuation, or kills the deal;
- AI adoption left to individual initiative, with no rules or measurement, or no adoption at all.

## What a fractional CTO changes

**The right level of experience.** A small company often couldn’t attract a CTO with 20 or 25 years in the field full-time. Fractional, it can.

**The right amount.** The company pays for the leadership days it needs, and adjusts the cadence as things change.

**An outside view.** A CTO who has seen other companies at the same stage spots problems earlier, and knows what worked elsewhere.

**A prepared hire.** When the company is ready for a full-time CTO, the fractional CTO knows exactly what profile to look for, joins the interviews, and onboards the successor.

**An AI transformation that gets led.** The fractional CTO finds the use cases, builds them with the teams, and runs an adoption plan, in engineering and elsewhere.

**No permanent commitment.** No long-term employment contract, no severance, no probation period to manage.

## Signs it’s time

- the founder spends more time on technical issues than on sales or product;
- no one can say for sure whether the engineering team is any good;
- releases keep taking longer, with no clear explanation;
- the company depends on a single developer or a single agency;
- a major technical hire is coming up;
- a fundraise, a sale, or a large customer demands precise technical answers;
- the CTO just left. In that case, see [interim CTO](/en-us/articles/what-is-an-interim-cto).

## When it’s the wrong answer

- **All you need is development capacity.** A freelancer or an agency will cost less.
- **The engineering team is already several dozen people.** Day-to-day leadership fills a week: you need a full-time CTO.
- **Leadership isn’t ready to delegate.** A CTO with no authority to decide changes nothing.

## Bottom line

For a small or mid-sized company, the question isn’t “do we need a CTO?” but “how much CTO do we need?” As long as the answer is less than a full week, fractional is the most effective option.
