## Why experience matters more here

An in-house CTO has months to learn their company. A fractional CTO has a few days a week. An interim CTO has to take control within weeks.

Either way, they have to read a situation quickly, and read it right. That doesn’t come from a training course. It comes from having seen similar situations many times, and having seen how they played out.

That’s what Nicolas Mussat brings with 25+ years in the field.

## Knowing what breaks first

A growing company always breaks in the same places, in a fairly predictable order: knowledge concentrated in two or three heads, tools stacked with no coherence, blurry lines between product and engineering, debt that slows every release, security pushed to later.

At MeilleursAgents, from the first server to a team of 30, then at Prose from its founding to 300 people, Nicolas Mussat built the systems and organizations that had to hold up through those phases. That experience means dealing with a problem before it becomes a crisis.

## Having seen the trends come and go

Over 25 years, the industry has gone through the early commercial web, the spread of open source, the cloud, mobile, SaaS, microservices, and now generative AI. Each wave produced lasting choices and regretted ones. AI is the fastest of them all, and the one with the widest gap between a demo and a successful deployment.

An experienced CTO doesn’t reject what’s new. They can tell a technology that solves your problem from one that solves the problem of a company ten times your size. That judgment avoids needless rebuilds and oversized architectures.

## Staying hands-on in the AI era

Twenty-five years in the field could lead to a pure-manager role. That’s exactly what to avoid today. With AI agents, a CTO has to understand the work in detail and be able to execute: analyze code, prototype an idea, build a tool.

Experience doesn’t replace that practice; it makes it more effective. Knowing what failed elsewhere means judging agents’ output faster, and knowing where not to trust them. [Read the article: the CTO in the AI era](/en-us/articles/cto-in-the-ai-era)

## Knowing when not to build

The safest answer is often existing software, a simple integration, or the decision to do nothing. A junior CTO builds by reflex. An experienced one knows what a custom system costs over five years: maintenance, security, dependence on whoever wrote it.

## Connecting technology and operations

Nicolas Mussat’s background spans technology, information systems, and operations. In a small or mid-sized company, these aren’t separate: an order, an invoice, or a support ticket runs through every system the company has.

A CTO who only looks at the product leaves part of the gains, and part of the risks, on the table.

## Talking to every audience

A CTO talks to engineers, a CEO, a board, investors, acquirers. Each expects a different answer, and none should get a polished version of reality. You learn that by having those conversations, for years.

## Building for what comes next

Two companies built from the inside teach one thing: the work that matters is the work that keeps running after you leave. That’s why every Monolithic Lab engagement plans its end from day one: decisions documented, team self-sufficient, successor hired or trained.

## Bottom line

A senior CTO’s experience shows in how fast and how accurately they decide in a situation that’s new to them. That’s exactly what a fractional role and an interim engagement demand.
